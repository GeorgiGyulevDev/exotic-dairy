About Exotic Dairy

Building a new rare-milk ecosystem.

Learn more about the company, its multidisciplinary expertise and the long-term vision behind Exotic Dairy.

Exotic Dairy is a Bulgarian research, development and production company focused on innovative products and processing technologies based on camel and donkey milk.

The company combines research, animal expertise, product development, technology and business partnerships to transform rare milk sources into premium and functional products.

Camel and donkey milk have nutritional, biological and technological characteristics that distinguish them from conventional dairy sources.

Their limited production, unusual protein composition and processing challenges have also prevented many potential applications from becoming mainstream dairy products. Exotic Dairy focuses on turning those challenges into opportunities through research, technology and specialized product development.

Exotic Dairy is built around research and product innovation rather than conventional high-volume dairy production.

Our model connects animal breeding and welfare, milk production, scientific research, processing technology, product development, testing, commercialization and market partnerships within one ecosystem.

Exotic Dairy brings together professionals across business strategy, finance, animal breeding, veterinary and livestock expertise, nutrition, food technology, research and product development.

The internal multidisciplinary team is complemented by external scientific and strategic expertise, allowing the company to approach rare milk from several interconnected disciplines rather than from a single production perspective.

Developing rare-milk products requires expertise across the entire value chain — from animal breeding, welfare, husbandry and nutrition to milk science, processing technology, functional nutrition, product development, commercialization and international partnerships.

This multidisciplinary structure allows Exotic Dairy to treat rare milk as an integrated ecosystem rather than simply another dairy ingredient.

Exotic Dairy aims to help build a new premium and functional rare-milk category based on science, responsible animal management, specialized technology and high-value products.

The long-term model combines production, research, education, commercial partnerships and international market development.

Camel Milk

A rare dairy source with distinctive characteristics.

Explore the nutritional profile, scientific interest and technological challenges associated with camel milk.

Camel milk has a broadly comparable basic nutritional composition to whole cow milk, but differs in several important respects.

Studies report differences in fatty-acid composition, protein structure, vitamins and minerals. Camel milk is frequently reported to contain higher vitamin C levels and may contain a greater proportion of unsaturated fatty acids.

Exact composition varies according to breed, diet, geography, lactation stage and production conditions.

Camel milk provides proteins, fats, lactose, vitamins and minerals, but its nutritional balance differs from conventional cow milk.

  • Vitamin C is often reported at comparatively higher levels.
  • It provides calcium, potassium, zinc and iron.
  • Its protein profile differs significantly from cow milk.
  • Its fatty-acid composition may contain a larger proportion of unsaturated fats.
  • It naturally contains bioactive proteins including lactoferrin, lysozyme and immunoglobulins.

Camel milk contains a different balance of caseins and whey proteins compared with cow milk.

One notable difference is that camel milk does not contain measurable β-lactoglobulin, one of the major whey proteins found in cow milk.

Camel milk also contains naturally occurring lactoferrin, immunoglobulins, lysozyme and other proteins that continue to attract scientific interest.

Some people report better tolerance of camel milk compared with cow milk. Differences in protein composition and fat structure may contribute to these observations.

Individual tolerance varies, however, and camel milk should not be regarded as universally easier to digest.

Camel milk has a different protein profile from cow milk and does not contain measurable β-lactoglobulin.

Research has therefore explored whether camel milk may be better tolerated by some people who react to cow-milk proteins.

People with diagnosed milk allergies should seek appropriate medical advice before introducing a new animal milk.

Yes. Camel milk naturally contains compounds including lactoferrin, lysozyme, immunoglobulins and other proteins that have been studied for their biological properties.

Laboratory and scientific research has investigated antimicrobial, antioxidant and immune-related activity associated with some of these components.

Yes. Camel milk has been studied in relation to glucose metabolism, insulin-like proteins and blood-glucose control.

Some clinical studies have reported potentially beneficial changes, but current evidence is not sufficient to consider camel milk a treatment for diabetes.

Camel milk must not replace insulin, prescribed medication or professional medical care.

Camel milk has been investigated in a limited number of studies involving autism spectrum disorder and selected neurological conditions.

Some small studies have reported changes in selected behavioral or biochemical measures, but the evidence remains limited and is not sufficient to establish camel milk as a treatment.

Further high-quality clinical research is required.

Pasteurized camel milk is the safer choice.

Like other raw animal milks, unpasteurized camel milk may carry pathogenic microorganisms and zoonotic risks. Controlled processing, hygiene and pasteurization are therefore important parts of safe commercial production.

When safely produced and properly processed, camel milk can be incorporated into a normal diet.

It can be consumed as drinking milk or used in fermented products, yogurt, labneh, cheese, desserts, confectionery, sauces, baking and other food applications.

Exotic Dairy focuses on products where the characteristics of camel milk can create meaningful nutritional, technological or premium-market value.

Camel milk remains a relatively rare raw material and is more difficult to produce and process at scale than conventional dairy milk.

Its protein and casein characteristics also make processes such as coagulation and cheese production technologically different from cow, goat, sheep or buffalo milk.

Limited supply, specialized processing requirements and a relatively young commercial infrastructure all contribute to its rarity in retail markets.

Conventional cheese-making methods cannot always be transferred directly from cow milk to camel milk.

Differences in casein composition and coagulation behavior can lead to slower or weaker curd formation. Specialized cultures, enzymes, process conditions and technological know-how may therefore be required.

The two main domesticated camel species are the one-humped dromedary (Camelus dromedarius) and the two-humped Bactrian camel (Camelus bactrianus).

Dromedaries account for most commercial camel milk production worldwide. Bactrian camels are also milked in some regions but generally have lower commercial milk availability.

The wild camel (Camelus ferus) is a separate endangered species and is not a commercial dairy animal.

Milk composition differs between species and also between breeds, environments, feeding systems and stages of lactation.

Bactrian camel milk is often reported to contain higher fat and protein concentrations, while dromedaries generally offer greater potential for commercial milk production.

Milk yield varies considerably according to species, breed, genetics, nutrition, management, lactation stage and environment.

Specialized dairy dromedaries can produce substantially more milk than animals managed under traditional extensive systems.

This variability is one reason breeding, nutrition and herd management are important parts of commercial camel dairy development.

Donkey Milk

Scarce, distinctive and technologically challenging.

Discover why donkey milk is attracting scientific and commercial interest despite remaining one of the world's rarest dairy raw materials.

Donkey milk is generally lighter and lower in calories than cow milk, with significantly less fat and protein, a higher lactose content and a different protein composition.

Its overall macronutrient pattern is often described as being somewhat closer to human milk than cow milk, although donkey milk is not equivalent to human breast milk.

Donkey milk shares several compositional characteristics with human milk, including relatively low fat and casein, higher lactose and a comparatively high proportion of whey proteins.

This similarity has attracted scientific interest, but donkey milk is not identical to human milk and should not automatically be considered a substitute for breast milk or regulated infant formula.

Donkey milk is naturally low in fat and energy, relatively high in lactose and has a substantially different protein profile from cow milk.

It also contains vitamin C and naturally occurring bioactive proteins such as lysozyme and lactoferrin.

Whether it is preferable depends on individual nutritional needs, tolerance and diagnosed allergies or intolerances.

Donkey milk has relatively low casein and a comparatively high proportion of whey proteins.

It also naturally contains proteins such as lysozyme and lactoferrin, which are among the reasons donkey milk continues to attract interest in nutrition and functional-food research.

Research suggests that donkey milk can be well tolerated by some people who cannot tolerate cow milk, particularly some children with confirmed cow-milk-protein allergy.

Clinical studies have reported high tolerance rates in selected groups of children challenged with donkey milk. These findings are promising but do not mean that donkey milk is universally tolerated.

The distinctive casein-to-whey balance and different protein profile of donkey milk have led researchers to investigate it as a possible alternative for some people with cow-milk-protein allergy.

Anyone with a diagnosed milk allergy should use new animal milks only under appropriate medical guidance.

No animal milk should be considered universally hypoallergenic.

Donkey milk may be tolerated by some individuals who react to cow milk, but individual responses differ and allergic reactions remain possible.

Donkey milk naturally contains bioactive proteins including lysozyme and lactoferrin.

These substances have been studied for antimicrobial and immune-modulating properties. Their presence does not mean that drinking donkey milk should be regarded as a treatment for infectious disease.

Donkey milk has a long history of traditional use in connection with whooping cough and respiratory complaints.

This historical use is scientifically interesting, but there is currently no convincing clinical evidence that donkey milk cures or treats pertussis.

There is currently no good clinical evidence that donkey milk can treat autism spectrum disorder or neurological conditions.

Research claims in this area are more often associated with camel milk, where several small human studies have been conducted. Even there, the evidence remains limited.

Pasteurized donkey milk is the safer choice.

Although donkey milk contains natural antimicrobial components, raw milk can still contain harmful microorganisms. Appropriate heat treatment substantially reduces microbiological risk.

Yes. When safely processed and commercially available, donkey milk can be incorporated into a normal diet.

Potential applications include pasteurized milk, yogurt, kefir and other fermented beverages, specialty cheeses, ice cream, chocolate and other premium products.

Donkey milk can be developed into pasteurized milk, fermented products, yogurt, kefir, specialty cheeses, confectionery, ice cream, chocolate and other premium food applications.

Powdered formats, naturally flavored products and selected functional applications may also be explored where technically and legally appropriate.

Donkey milk remains rare primarily because of production volume, economics, processing difficulty, logistics, regulation and animal-management requirements.

Donkeys produce much smaller quantities of milk than conventional dairy animals, while responsible milking systems must also consider animal welfare and the needs of the foal.

These factors make donkey milk a naturally scarce and premium raw material.

Donkey milk forms a relatively weak curd compared with cow, sheep or goat milk because of differences in casein composition and concentration.

Conventional cheese-making methods therefore cannot always be transferred directly. Specialized cultures, enzymes and processing technologies may be required.

Donkeys number in the tens of millions worldwide, with large populations across Africa, Asia and parts of Latin America.

Despite the global population, only a small proportion of donkeys are managed specifically for organized dairy production, which helps explain why donkey milk remains scarce.
